## Background :

Anthropogenic activities, such as urbanization and agricultural intensification, significantly alter ecosystems and threaten biodiversity. Among these threats, habitat fragmentation and the introduction of various pollutants, including noise pollution, stand out as critical challenges. These disruptions alter the natural dynamics of ecosystems, often with cascading effects on species interactions and ecological stability. Noise pollution, in particular, disrupts species that rely heavily on vocal communication or echolocation for survival. Birds and bats are especially vulnerable, as their ability to communicate, navigate, and forage is affected by elevated noise levels.  

Bats, as bioindicators, are especially sensitive to these changes, highlighting the importance of preserving their habitats against such disturbances. Their reliance on echolocation makes them highly susceptible to acoustic interference, emphasizing the critical need to preserve and restore their habitats. Protecting bat populations not only aids in maintaining ecosystem balance but also serves as a broader indicator of efforts to mitigate anthropogenic impacts on biodiversity.

## Material and Method :

To study the impact of noise pollution on bat activity, we used ultrasound detectors to record signals emitted by bats, which serve as indicators of their activity. These detectors were placed at three different distances from the noise source in two areas where noise-heavy shows are regularly held. Recordings were conducted on the day before, the day of, and the day after the shows took place. Additionally, we accounted for several climatic variables, including wind speed, lunar phases, and temperature.                                                                                                                      

## Results :

The results of our study reveal that distance, species, time, and interactions between nights and distance significantly influence the number of bats detected. In an initial model based solely on the number of bat detections, without including the species detected, it is predicted that each additional unit of distance reduces the total number of detections by 16.3% (p = 0.047). Furthermore, the nights following disturbances show an increase of approximately 55.2% in the number of individual detections (p = 0.050). Additionally, nights with noise disturbance show a trend toward a decrease in the number of bat detections by about 34.2% compared to nights without disturbance, though this effect remains marginally significant (p = 0.064). However, at 470 meters, there is a significant increase in the number of detections during disturbed nights (+295%, p < 0.001) and the following nights (+124%, p = 0.003).
